- You need a decision paper your board will take seriously.

Sound familiar?

- You cannot tell how much of the demand is real.
- You do not know who you would really be competing against, or at what price.
- Subsidiary, joint venture or partner: everyone has an opinion and none of them agree.
- The ambition is clear, but the plan is not.
- You suspect there are rules for your sector that nobody has told you about yet.

What we do

## The work, in plain terms

Practical work, in plain language, from the first question to a decision you can defend.

- Market size, demand and growth: what is really there
- Who you would compete with, and how they win
- Which customers to go after first, and why they would choose you
- Your entry options side by side, with our recommendation
- The rules and licences that will affect you, flagged early
- A phased launch roadmap with a first-year budget outline

Setting up a business in a new country is mostly paperwork, patience and knowing who to ask. We coordinate the whole thing so you can get on with running the business.

**[ Book a Consultation ]**

Service overview

## A proper start in Saudi Arabia

We help you choose the right structure, prepare the documents, deal with the relevant authorities and keep the process moving. You will always know where things stand, what is waiting on whom, and what comes next. We coordinate the work alongside your own lawyers and accountants; we do not replace them.

Who it is for

## Is this the right help for you?

- You are an international business entering the Saudi market.
- You are an established company opening a local entity or subsidiary.
- You want a compliant start that does not lose months to avoidable delays.
- You want one team to coordinate everything from incorporation to opening day.

Sound familiar?

## Problems we help you solve

- The licensing and regulatory requirements feel like a maze.
- You are not sure which legal structure suits your goals.
- Several government bodies are involved and nobody seems to talk to each other.
- Documents and approvals take longer than anyone told you.

- Preparing and submitting incorporation documents
- Sector-specific approvals and requirements
- Working with service providers and other stakeholders
- Liaison with the relevant government authorities

# Seven questions to answer before you enter Saudi Arabia

A practical guide to defining, testing and de-risking your move into the Saudi market.

Saudi Arabia is full of opportunity, and that is exactly the problem. When a market looks this promising, it is easy to move quickly on enthusiasm and sort out the details later. Later is when it gets expensive.

So before a business commits, we ask it seven questions. None of them is clever. All of them are easy to skip. If you can answer them honestly, you are in a much stronger position than most.

01

## What are you trying to achieve here?

Revenue? A long-term base? A gateway to the wider region? A piece of a global strategy? These lead to different decisions about structure, investment and timing, so it pays to be specific.

A useful test: can you explain your goal in two sentences, to someone who has never heard of your business?

02

## Who exactly will buy from you?

“Saudi consumers” or “Saudi businesses” is not an answer. Which ones? What do they need that they are not getting? How do they buy: who decides, and how long does it take?

The best answers come from talking to people like them, before you build anything.

03

## Who are you up against?

Look at both local and international players. What do they do well? Where are they weak? What do customers say about them?

You are not looking for a reason to give up. You are looking for the gap that you can fill better than anyone else.

04

## Which way in is right for you?

A wholly owned company, a joint venture, a distributor, a strategic partner. Each gives you a different mix of control, cost, speed and risk.

There is rarely a perfect option. The right one is the one whose trade-offs you can live with.

05

## How will you price and position yourself?

Prices that work elsewhere may be too high or too low here once you add import costs, partner margins and what local competitors charge. Positioning is the same: what feels premium in one market can feel ordinary in another.

06

## Who will you work with?

Partners, distributors, suppliers, advisers. The right ones bring knowledge, networks and capability you would take years to build. The wrong ones are a long and costly lesson.

Know what you want from a partner before you meet one, and check them as carefully as you would check a bank.

07

## Is your own organisation ready?

Is your leadership truly committed, or is this an experiment? Do you have the people, the budget and the patience? Entering Saudi Arabia works best as a long-term decision, with room to adapt.

If you are not sure, our free Readiness Scorecard is a good way to find out.

## To sum up

Answering these seven questions will not remove every uncertainty. It will focus your effort, reduce the risks you can avoid, and give your leadership a sound basis for a decision.

If you would like to talk any of them through, we would be glad to.

# Why your home-market pricing will not work here

Practical thoughts on aligning your offer and your price with how Saudi customers see value.

Many international businesses assume that what works at home will work here. Sometimes it does. More often it needs adjusting, and the signs show up quickly: sales calls that stall at price, margins that are thinner than planned, or customers who like the product but do not buy.

01

## Start with the customer, not the price list

Decide which customers you want to win and what they care about most: quality, service, brand, speed, relationships. Price should reflect that value. It should not simply be your global rate card converted into riyals.

02

## Learn what customers compare you with

Customers anchor on what similar offers cost, locally and internationally, including discounts and payment terms. Find those anchors before you set your own price.

03

## Add up the real cost of serving them

Import duties, logistics, localisation, partner margins and after-sales support all affect the price you can sustain. Build them into your model now, not after the first invoice.

04

## Test, learn, adjust

Start with a focused pilot. Watch where deals are won and lost, listen to why, and refine your price and positioning before you scale. Treat your first price as a hypothesis, not a decision.

## To sum up

Pricing and positioning are not one-off decisions. Treat them as things to test in the market, with clear measures for what success looks like.

# How to choose a partner in Saudi Arabia (and how not to)

Key criteria and practical steps for assessing and selecting commercial partners.

Partnerships are often the quickest way to customers, licences and local credibility. They are also among the hardest decisions to undo. So choosing well matters more than choosing fast.

01

## Know what you need before you meet anyone

Market access? Regulatory standing? Distribution? Capital? Credibility? Be specific, because your criteria follow from the need. Without them, every charming person looks like a good partner.

02

## Check capability and track record

Ask for references and call them. Look at the brands and customers they already serve, their financial standing, and the depth of the team who would actually work with you.

03

## Test whether your interests line up

Do the incentives point the same way? Are expectations on exclusivity and long-term ambition compatible? Raise these before anyone drafts terms, while it is still easy to talk.

04

## Agree how it works, and how it ends

Reporting, who decides what, how performance is measured, and what happens if you part ways. Agree these at the start, with advice from your own lawyers, while everyone still likes each other.

## To sum up

A disciplined approach, led by criteria rather than introductions, shortens the road to the right partner and saves you from avoidable surprises.

# Map the rules before you commit

How to work out early which licences and approvals apply to your business.

When entry plans slip, the cause is rarely a rule that was too hard. It is a rule nobody knew about until it was in the way. A little mapping early on avoids most of that.

01

## List the authorities that matter

Commercial, sector and local authorities may all be involved. Write down who they are, what each one approves, and what they will ask you for.

02

## Put the approvals in a realistic order

Some approvals depend on others. Build those dependencies into your timeline, with a sensible buffer, and see what it does to your launch date.

03

## Confirm with licensed advisers

Requirements change. Always confirm the current rules with your licensed legal and tax advisers, and the relevant authority, before you act.

## To sum up

Treat regulation as part of the plan from the first day, not a hurdle to clear after the decision is made.

## Sector notes
- **fmcg**: In consumer goods, shelf access and distributor relationships usually decide the pace, so partner quality and first-year working capital deserve extra attention.
- **real-estate**: In real estate, regulation, land and the choice of delivery partners shape everything. Check approvals and contractor capacity early.
- **hospitality**: In hospitality, talent and operating standards are the hard part. Plan your hiring and training before you plan your opening date.
- **manufacturing**: In manufacturing, site, incentives and supply chain come first. Localisation targets and skilled labour can set your timeline.
- **technology-digital**: In technology, who the buyer is (enterprise or government) and how they procure will shape your sales cycle. Data and hosting questions often surface early.
- **construction-engineering**: In construction, project pipelines, prequalification and partner capacity matter. Cash flow across long payment cycles is a classic pressure point.
- **healthcare-life-sciences**: In healthcare, regulatory approvals and clinical or product standards come first. Allow for longer lead times than in most sectors.
- **logistics-supply-chain**: In logistics, location, customs and network partners decide your economics. Test the routes, not just the plan.
- **retail-ecommerce**: In retail and e-commerce, customer expectations on delivery, payment and Arabic experience are high. Get these right before you scale marketing.
- **professional-business-services**: In professional services, reputation and relationships are your product. Plan how you will build credibility before you scale the team.
- **tourism-leisure**: In tourism and leisure, seasonality, destination partners and licensing shape your plan. Pilot with a small group before you invest in scale.
