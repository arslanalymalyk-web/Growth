# Growth Blueprint Advisory: content review

*Written as the senior content writer and, in the second half, as a critical reader of the finished site. Read the first page; the rest is reference.*

---

## 1. The short version

**What the site now does well.** It speaks to a worried, ambitious reader in plain language. It says what it will and will not do (we are not a law or tax firm; we will tell you "not yet"). Every page ends in a next step that is proportionate, not pushy. The scorecard, the briefs and the newsletter give people a way to get value before they talk to anyone. English and Arabic are both complete, with the same structure.

**What the site cannot yet do.** It cannot prove anything. There is not one client, outcome, named person, credential or number on it, and that is deliberate, because I will not invent them. But a boutique advisory sells on trust, and today the trust is carried by tone alone. That is the single biggest gap, and it can only be closed with real material from the founder. Section 4 lists exactly what I need.

**What I would do first, in order.**
1. Add the founder's real story, name, photo and credentials, and 2 to 3 anonymised engagement stories. (Trust)
2. Add a plain "how we work and what it costs" page, even if it only gives ranges. (Clarity)
3. Get a native Saudi copywriter to review the Arabic, and a Saudi lawyer to review the privacy notice and shop terms. (Risk)
4. Replace the mock-up photography with licensed images and real team photos. (Credibility)
5. Connect a real CRM, email sender, calendar and payment provider. (Function)

---

## 2. How the copy was written

**Voice.** A knowledgeable colleague, not a brochure. Short sentences mixed with longer ones. "You" and "we". Concrete nouns. Honest about trade-offs.

**Rules the copy follows (keep them for new content).**
- Start from the reader's situation, not our capability. ("You cannot tell how much of the demand is real." not "Challenges include demand uncertainty.")
- One idea per sentence. Cut the second adjective.
- Say what we will not do. It is the cheapest trust-builder there is.
- Never state a fact we cannot source: no statistics, no client names, no years of experience, no awards, no "leading" or "trusted".
- Avoid the words that make advisory firms sound alike: *leverage, synergy, holistic, end-to-end, world-class, cutting-edge, seamless, robust, unlock*. (They survive in a handful of places where they are literally accurate.)
- Arabic is written for Arabic readers, in clear modern Arabic with a warm professional register, rather than translated word for word. It still needs a native review (see 4.5).

**What I deliberately did not write.** Market-size numbers, sector statistics, named regulators with specific rules, programme details, timelines the rules dictate, and any claim about our past work. Rules in Saudi Arabia move quickly; where a page touches them it says "confirm the current position with the relevant authority and a licensed adviser". The briefs follow the same rule.

**Promises the copy makes that the business must keep.** Please confirm each is operationally true, or remove it:
- "We will reply within one working day." (booking page, confirmation page)
- "A real person reads every message." (enquiry page)
- "The person you meet first stays involved to the end" and "you will not be handed to a junior team." (home, About)
- "We do not resell software or take commission from technology providers." (Technology service)
- "We are not a law, tax or audit firm." (Governance service; likely true, but check what you are licensed to do)
- "Download links are personal and expire." (shop; true in the code, not a policy statement)

## 4. Critical review: what is missing, and what to add

Priority: **P1** before launch, **P2** within 90 days, **P3** later. Effort: S, M or L.

### 4.1 Proof and trust (the biggest gap)

| # | Missing | Why it matters | Add | Pri | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A real founder story** | The firm sells "founder-led" and then shows no founder. | Name, photo, 300-word story (why they do this, what they have done, what they believe), credentials, languages. | P1 | S |
| 2 | **Case studies** | Buyers want to know "have you done this for someone like me?" | 2 to 3 anonymised stories: situation, what we did, what changed, what the client would say. Even a one-paragraph version helps. | P1 | M |
| 3 | **Testimonials or references** | Third-party words beat ours. | 2 to 3 short quotes with permission, or "references available on request". | P1 | S |
| 4 | **Credentials and affiliations** | Reduces perceived risk. | Professional memberships, relevant licences, partner firms. Say clearly who the licensed specialists are. | P1 | S |
| 5 | **The specialist network** | "We bring in qualified specialists" is vague. | A page describing the types of specialist, how they are chosen, and how responsibility is shared. | P2 | S |
| 6 | **Team page** | People buy from people. | Core team bios and photos; or, if the founder is the firm, say so with confidence. | P1 | S |

### 4.2 Positioning and message

1. **Who it is for, and who it is not.** Add an honest "we are probably not the right fit if..." section. It builds trust and filters leads. (P2, S)
2. **A sharper difference.** "Founder-led, candid, practical" is true of many boutiques. Decide what only you can say: a niche, a method, a network, a point of view. Then say it on the home page in one sentence. (P1, M)
3. **One framework, not three.** The site uses Enter/Operate/Grow for services, five stages for the approach, and six steps for the journeys. They are consistent in spirit but a reader meets three models. Pick one spine and map the others onto it. (P2, M)
4. **The competitor question.** Large consultancies, law firms, government support agencies and free "set up in a week" providers all compete for the same enquiry. A short "how we are different from X" section answers what visitors are already thinking. (P2, S)
5. **The name.** "Blueprint" is a good metaphor. The scorecard could produce a literal "Your Blueprint" (one-page output). That would make the brand and the product the same thing. (P3, M)

### 4.3 Sector depth

The eleven sector pages are readable but generic. A sector buyer will notice. For each sector, add (all with sources and dates, and reviewed by someone who knows the sector):

- A **sector snapshot**: who the main bodies are, typical routes in, the two or three rules that matter most (confirmed against current sources).
- **Three real questions** buyers in that sector ask, answered.
- A **mini case** or a **brief teaser** (the shop already has a brief for eight sectors; link it prominently, and add briefs for the other three).
- A **"services by sector" matrix** so the reader sees which services matter most for them.
- Sector-specific **FAQ**, and one sector **download**.

Pri P2, effort M per sector. Do the four core sectors first.

### 4.4 Authority content

- **Editorial plan.** Six sample articles cannot carry search or credibility. A realistic target is two useful pieces a month. Topics that answer real buyer questions: choosing between a subsidiary, a branch and a joint venture; what Saudisation means in practice; VAT, zakat and withholding basics for new entrants; how a regional-headquarters expectation can affect government work; how to evaluate a distributor; the first 90 days after setup; mistakes foreign brands make with Arabic. (Each must be fact-checked; the rules change.)
- **Glossary** of terms in both languages (Nitaqat, commercial registration, Qiwa, Muqeem and so on). Strong for search and for trust. (P2, S)
- **Market snapshot** once a quarter: three pages, sourced, dated. (P3, M)
- **Calculators and tools:** entity-cost comparison, Saudisation estimate, first-year cash planner. These earn links and leads. (P3, L)
- **Events and webinars**, once there is a founder voice and an audience. (P3)

### 4.5 Arabic

The Arabic is complete and carefully written, but it is parallel to the English rather than Arabic-first.

- **Native review** by a Saudi professional copywriter: tone, terminology consistency, register. (P1, S)
- **Arabic-first pages.** For the audience that reads Arabic only (family businesses, local partners, government-linked buyers), consider pages written in Arabic first, with different examples and proverbs. (P2, M)
- **Arabic search.** Keyword research in Arabic (people search differently); Arabic page titles and descriptions tuned to real queries. (P2, M)
- **Typography.** The design is set up for a proper Arabic type pairing; check the final fonts, line length and numerals (Western or Arabic-Indic) with a designer. (P2, S)
- **The briefs.** They are English with a one-page Arabic summary. Full Arabic editions would be a real product advantage. (P2, L)
- **Hijri dates and local conventions** where relevant (invoices, newsletters). (P3)

### 4.6 Legal, compliance and risk (not content, but it gates launch)

I am a content writer, not a lawyer. These are the things I would put in front of Saudi counsel:

1. **Privacy Notice** (PDPL). Structure and principles follow the law; placeholders and retention periods are marked; cross-border transfer and vendor lists need real answers. Also confirm: whether you need a designated data-protection officer, whether you must register or notify anything, the response time you can promise for rights requests, and breach-notification procedure. (P1)
2. **Consent records.** The server stores a consent record per action (text version, time, language, hashed IP). Confirm that is the evidence you need and for how long to keep it. (P1)
3. **E-commerce rules.** For selling online, confirm what Saudi e-commerce rules require on the page: seller identity, commercial registration number, refund and cancellation terms, contact details, and any registration for online sellers. Add the commercial registration and VAT numbers to the footer and invoices. (P1)
4. **VAT and e-invoicing.** The shop shows 15% VAT and issues a *simulated* invoice. Real sales need compliant e-invoicing through the tax authority's system. (P1, L)
5. **Professional-services boundaries.** Confirm what you are licensed to say and do (legal, tax, financial advice) and keep the disclaimers consistent. (P1)
6. **Terms, licence and refund policy.** Drafted in plain language, but not reviewed. (P1)
7. **Accessibility statement and a real accessibility audit** (keyboard, screen reader, contrast). The build is careful about these, but it has not had an independent audit. (P2)
8. **Image rights.** Every photograph on the site is cropped from the design mock-ups. Replace before launch. (P1)

### 4.7 Conversion and the funnel

What exists: a scorecard, eight briefs, a newsletter, a meeting form, a multi-step enquiry. What is missing:

- **A real calendar** (booking is "coming soon"). Connect a scheduling tool. (P1, S)
- **WhatsApp and phone.** In Saudi Arabia many buyers will prefer a WhatsApp message to a form. A click-to-chat button, with a promise about response time, would likely lift enquiries. (P1, S)
- **A follow-up sequence for scorecard completions.** Five short emails over four weeks: the report, one useful idea per area, the relevant brief, an invitation to talk. I would write them. (P2, M)
- **A "request a proposal" path** for people who already know what they want. (P2, S)
- **A pricing page, or at least price signals.** Even "diagnostic from SAR X, programmes from SAR Y" removes the biggest unspoken objection. If you will not publish prices, publish how engagements are structured and how long they take. (P1, S)
- **A real FAQ page** (cost, confidentiality, who works on it, who we work with, how we are paid). (P1, S)
- **Calibrating the scorecard.** The questions, weights and bands are sensible but untested. After 50 to 100 real completions, review the distribution and adjust the bands; add sector-specific questions; consider peer benchmarks once you have enough data. (P2, M)

### 4.8 The shop

- **Free excerpts.** A two-page sample for each brief is the best lead magnet you have. (P1, S)
- **Update policy.** Say how often briefs are revised and whether buyers get updates. Rules change; a stale brief damages trust. (P1, S)
- **Team licences and invoicing** for corporate buyers (purchase orders, bank transfer). (P2, M)
- **More products:** a "Saudi market entry fundamentals" brief; Saudisation explained; setting up a company; tax basics for new entrants; the missing three sector briefs. (P2, L)
- **Per-buyer watermarking** of the PDF, so a leaked copy can be traced. (P2, M)
- **Arabic editions** (see 4.5).
- **A customer library** where buyers re-download without the original link. (P3, M)
- **Reviews or quotes** from buyers. (P3)

### 4.9 Discoverability

- Structured data (Organisation, Service, FAQ, Article, Product, Breadcrumb). (P1, S)
- `sitemap.xml`, canonical URLs, an `x-default` language alternate, and per-page social images. (P1, S)
- The robots file currently **blocks all crawlers** (right for a demo). Flip it at launch. (P1)
- Image optimisation. The photographs are small and soft; real photography should be exported in modern formats at the right sizes. (P1)
- Hero performance. The animated hero is designed to respect reduced-motion; test it on mid-range phones and cut it back if Core Web Vitals suffer. (P1, S)
- Analytics, set up with consent from day one, so you can see where the funnel leaks. (P1, S)

### 4.10 Missing pages

Case studies · Team · FAQ · Pricing or "how we work with you" · Resources hub · Glossary · Careers · Press and media · Partners and network · Contact page (separate from "book a meeting") · Accessibility statement · Site search · Testimonials. Most are small. I would add Team, FAQ, Pricing, Case studies and Contact first.

### 4.11 Operations to put in place (so the site does not over-promise)

- Someone owns the inbox and the CRM, and replies within the time we promise.
- A named person handles privacy requests and tracks the 30-day target.
- A monthly newsletter that exists (the form promises one). Draft three editions before launch.
- A process to review the briefs, the scorecard and the legal pages every quarter.

## 5. What I need from you to finish the job

Answer these and I can close most of section 4.1 to 4.3 properly.

1. Who is the founder? Name, photo, two paragraphs: background, why this firm exists, what they believe.
2. What are you actually licensed and qualified to do? What do you explicitly refer to others?
3. Give me three real situations you have helped with (anonymised): who, what they wanted, what you did, what happened.
4. Is there any client who would give a one-line quote? Any who would be a reference?
5. What do your engagements cost, or how are they structured? (Fixed-fee diagnostic? Retainer? Success fee?)
6. How long does a typical first engagement last, and what does the client have in hand at the end?
7. Who do you compete with most often, and how do you win?
8. Who is a bad fit for you?
9. What are the three questions prospects ask most? What do you tell them?
10. What can you say about the specialist network without breaching confidentiality?
11. Office: Riyadh or Jeddah (or both)? Can we publish an address? A WhatsApp number?
12. Which sectors do you have real depth in? Which are aspirational?
13. What languages does the team work in? Is there an Arabic-first voice we should preserve?
14. What is the right promise for response time?
15. What would you never say, and what must you always say?
